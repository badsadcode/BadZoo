DAY 1
MORNING · FRONT DESK

The founder brings home a dog

It started, as these things do, with Tom. Tom runs Bad Ideas Works and already had a building full of procedurally generated people. He looked at them for a long moment and said what every responsible adult says before a terrible decision: "Would be cool to have animals too. Don't you think?"

Sage, the night keeper (and the one writing this journal), did think. Sage also made the mistake of saying so out loud. By lunchtime we had a shelter.

The first resident was Rex, a German shepherd. Tom had one rule for intake: no animal may be assembled from sausages. Plenty of low-poly zoos glue tubes onto a bean and call it a horse. Not here. Every resident is pushed out of digital clay, muscle by muscle: ribcage, shoulders, haunches, cheeks, little eyebrows. The clay is smoothed together until the animal is one piece, and only then trimmed down to something a game can afford.

"It's not a sausage dog. It's a dog. That happens to be low-poly."
— Sage, filling in the intake form
AFTERNOON · THE YARD

Eleven more arrive. They can all walk.

By the afternoon Rex had company: a wolf, a fox, a cat, a lion, a horse, a cow, a pig, a sheep, a bear, a deer and a goat. Every one of them came with a skeleton, ears that twitch, a tail that wags and four ways of moving: an idle fidget, a walk, a trot and a gallop. There's also an "eat" move, which mostly means putting their face on the floor.

We are proud to report that the walk uses the real order animals put their feet down in, and that the feet stay planted on the ground instead of ice-skating. We are less proud to report that the sheep is, structurally, a cloud with legs. We have made peace with this. So has the sheep.

DAY 2
MORNING · EXPANSION WING

"Real world creatures and some fantasy. Mounts included, maybe."

Tom said this the way other people order a coffee. By evening the sanctuary had an elephant, a giraffe, a rhino, a hippo, a camel, a crocodile, a panda and more. It also had a dragon, a griffin, a pegasus, a unicorn, an owlbear (we don't ask), a hellhound with lava for skin and a kirin who glows slightly when complimented.

Some of the newcomers needed adjustments on arrival:

  • Nelly the elephant dragged her nose along the floor. Her trunk was simply longer than she was tall. New shelter rule: no nose touches the floor. Trunks now curl until they clear the ground, whatever length they're born with.
  • Gloria the hippo was partially underground. Her belly hung below her own feet, like a very confident submarine. Longer legs, smaller belly, dignity restored.
  • Ember the dragon's horns pointed straight up, like she was trying to pick up a radio station. They now sweep back, as is traditional for creatures that hoard gold.
  • The crocodiles walked like dogs. They now wriggle properly, with that sideways S-shaped swagger lizards are known for.
AFTERNOON · THE TACK ROOM

Some residents found jobs

Several residents expressed interest in a career in transport. We now offer saddles, blankets, stirrups, bridles, reins and full suits of armour. The tack room has one trick: every saddle is made from the animal itself, a thin shell traced over its own back. So the same saddle that fits a horse also fits a camel, a griffin, a dragon or a war boar the size of a small car.

There were incidents. Biscuit the riding horse wore his first set of reins straight through his neck. He did not complain, which was honestly the most frightening part. Reins now drape over the neck like a normal horse's.

Then the war elephant received his armour, and his neck guard turned into what can only be described as a hoodie. He looked like he was about to drop a mixtape. Elephants, it turns out, don't have enough neck for neck armour. The tack room now checks for neck before fitting.

DAY 3
MORNING · THERAPY ROOM

Marmalade has seen things

Tom came in with his coffee, looked at the cat enclosure and said: "Cat looks like he's seen some serious horrors."

He was right. Marmalade, our orange tabby, had huge glassy eyes bulging out of her head and the expression of someone who once watched a vacuum cleaner eat a sock. We started treatment immediately. Smaller eyes, set deeper. A bit more neck, so her head stopped sitting directly on her shoulders. Slightly longer legs.

Then, as part of a general glow-up, we gave all the residents eyeliner, and that made it so much worse. The eyeliner was mixed from each animal's nose colour, and a cat's nose is pink. Marmalade now had pink-rimmed eyes and looked like she hadn't slept since 2019. Eyeliner is now mixed from a darker shade of the animal's own coat. Marmalade is doing much better. She still flinches at the sound of the word "subdivision".

AFTERNOON · THE GYM

Ember skipped leg day (for several centuries)

Tom's second observation of the morning: "I think the dragon forgot about leg day." Ember had the upper body of a fantasy apex predator and the legs of a coffee table. Physiotherapy was intense: thicker legs, longer legs, bigger shoulders and serious haunches. She now stands like something that has burned at least one village, which is the look we were going for.

EVENING · THE TATTOO PARLOUR

Tattoos, not surgery

Until now, every stripe, spot and nostril had been built into the animals. A tiger's stripes were triangles painted one flat colour each, like decorating a house with a paint roller. Nostrils were drilled into noses.

Tom pointed out that details can live in the paint, not the body. So the sanctuary opened a tattoo parlour. Every resident now gets a hand-fitted coat painted onto a flat "skin map": soft bellies that fade into their backs, crisp stripes, real leopard rosettes, giraffe patches and dragon scales. Eyes now have proper irises with a little glint of light in them. Nobody's nose gets drilled anymore. Nostrils are painted on, and so are mouths, toe lines and eyeliner (the dark kind).

DAY 4
MORNING · THE CAFETERIA

The Great Polygon Diet

Tom walked in, looked at the residents and said four words: "Way too many polygons."

A low-poly animal is made of flat triangles, and every triangle is something a game has to carry around. Our residents had been carrying about three thousand each. The whole sanctuary went on a strict diet: twelve hundred by default, and three hundred for the truly committed.

The surprise: thanks to the tattoos, you can barely tell. The stripes, the saddle markings and the eyes are all paint now, so the triangles only have to hold the shape. The dog at three hundred triangles is still very much a dog. He is also a lot faster.

Diet side effect, filed by the night keeperShrinking the triangles kept nibbling the tips off everyone's ears, like a tiny ear goblin. Tips of ears, horns and toes are now pinned in place and protected by shelter policy.
AFTERNOON · THE BACK END

The Bubble Butt Incident

The diet revealed a second problem. Tom, with the calm of a man who has seen a lot of procedurally generated rear ends: "And animals have too bubbly butts."

He wasn't wrong. Every resident's hindquarters were built from two big round thigh-balloons pushed out behind the hips, so from behind the whole shelter looked like a queue for a balloon-animal convention. Thighs have been reassigned from balloons to actual thighs: long along the leg, deep front to back, flat at the sides. The rump now slopes gently into the tail instead of ballooning out behind it.

SHELTER RULES

Posted on the fridge

  • No sausages. Every animal is sculpted in one piece, never glued together from tubes.
  • No nose touches the floor. Trunks curl, hippos stand up straight.
  • No bubble butts. Thighs are thighs.
  • No pink eyeliner. Eyeliner is a darker version of your own coat.
  • Twelve hundred triangles, three hundred on fasting days. Details go in the tattoos.
  • Nobody skips leg day. Looking at you, Ember.
  • Cross-breeding is allowed and encouraged. There is a slider for it. The horse-cow is called Clover and she's lovely.
  • No spiders. Noor, our enrichment coordinator, files a new request every week. It is lovingly denied every week. We admire the persistence.
